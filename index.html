Part One

The World in Your Cup

Before a bean reaches an Australian café, it crosses oceans. Coffee is one of the most traded agricultural commodities on earth — and Australia sits at the end of a supply chain that spans 50+ countries.

Where Australia's Coffee Comes From

The map below traces the flow of green coffee beans into Australia. A dense band across Latin America — led by Brazil, Colombia and Honduras — dominates, supplying over 60% of all imports. East Africa and South-East Asia fill the rest. Grey countries export nothing to Australia at all. Melbourne's café identity, for all its local pride, rests on a supply chain that could be disrupted by a drought on the other side of the world.

Not All Beans Are Equal

Two species dominate global coffee supply: Arabica and Robusta. Arabica — mild, aromatic, preferred by specialty cafés — commands the vast majority of global domestic consumption. Robusta is cheaper, more bitter, and holds its own in espresso blends and instant coffee.

Together they make up nearly all of the world's coffee supply. The preference gap between them tells you a great deal about how the world — and Australia — takes its coffee.

~70% of global consumption is Arabica
~30% Robusta — espresso blends & instant

Top 10 Coffee Bean Exporters, 2025

Brazil leads global green coffee bean exports with 37,000 thousand 60 kg bags — roughly 31% of all global supply. Vietnam follows in second, shipping 24,600 thousand bags of mostly Robusta. Colombia rounds out the top three at 11,500 thousand bags.

Together, the top three account for nearly 62% of all global exports. Indonesia rises notably to fourth place, while African exporters Ethiopia and Uganda complete the top six — bringing rich Arabica varieties that increasingly appear on Australian café menus.

"A crop failure in Minas Gerais, Brazil can send coffee prices across the world surging within a season."

Coffee Consumption by Country

How much coffee does the world actually drink? The choropleth below maps total consumption in 2023 — darker means more. The United States leads at 1.4 million tonnes, followed by Brazil (remarkably, also one of the world's biggest consumers of its own export) and Germany. Australia sits at roughly 47,000 tonnes — about 1.8 kg per person — a figure that understates the cultural intensity of its café scene.

Part Two

The Culture

The beans land in Australia and meet a country unlike most others. Nowhere here has turned coffee into quite such a defining feature of daily life — a ritual built suburb by suburb, state by state.

What Australia Orders

Walk into any café in the country and the story is clear: milk-based espresso drinks dominate. The latte leads at around 23%, with Americano with Milk and Cappuccino close behind. The flat white — an Australian invention that conquered London and New York — is not far off.

The "Others" category hints at a growing curiosity for specialty and non-traditional drinks. Espresso and Cortado remain niche despite strong cultural roots in Italian-Australian communities.

Australia's Café Footprint

Each dot below represents a cluster of cafés and restaurants in Australia, sized by the number of venues per state. The picture is immediate: NSW and Victoria dominate, together accounting for over 60% of all venues. The density in coastal urban centres tells the story of where coffee culture is most embedded — while the NT's sparse dot reflects both geography and population.

How Australia Searches for Coffee

Google Trends data from 2004 to 2026 tracks search interest in "coffee" and "café" across Australia. The lines reveal the rhythm of a culture: steady baseline interest punctuated by seasonal peaks, a clear surge in specialty coffee interest from around 2015, and a notable dip during the COVID lockdowns of 2020 — when café culture moved briefly indoors and online.

Part Three

The Price of the Ritual

Follow the bean all the way to its destination — the cup in your hand at 8am — and three things become clear: who drinks it, what it costs, and why the price keeps climbing.

Caffeine Intake Across Generations

Not all Australians drink the same amount of caffeine from sweetened beverages. The bubble chart below plots average daily intake by age group — bubble size encodes each group's share of total caffeine consumption. Young adults aged 18–29 lead at 21 mg/day, while teens consume surprising amounts relative to their size. Older Australians, loyal to their flat whites and long blacks, draw their caffeine from traditional brewed coffee rather than sweetened drinks.

The Rising Cost of a Cup

The annual average price of green coffee beans — converted to AUD — tells a volatile story. Supply shocks from the 1977 Brazilian frost, the 1997 Asian demand boom, and the 2011 global commodity spike each drove sharp price surges. But nothing compares to the 2024–25 period: a devastating drought and frost season in Brazil wiped out nearly half the global arabica crop, pushing AUD prices to record highs.

The dashed trend line smooths out the noise — the long-run direction is unmistakably upward. A crop that cost under $1 AUD per pound in the early 2000s now exceeds $5.

What a Flat White Costs Across Australia

The flat white is Australia's benchmark coffee — and its price varies more than you might expect. Darwin leads at $5.35, almost $0.45 above the cheapest state. New South Wales, despite hosting the most cafés in the country, comes in as the most affordable at $4.89.

The diverging bar chart at left shows each state's deviation from the national average of $4.99. Victoria sits at the exact average — fitting for a state that defines the national coffee standard. The NT's premium reflects the cost of doing business remotely: higher freight, fewer competitors, and a smaller customer base.

Flat White Price, City by City

Zoom in further and the city-level picture adds texture. Within states, prices are often consistent — suggesting state-level cost structures (wages, rent, supply logistics) dominate local pricing. The strip chart plots individual cities, jittered to reveal clustering. Darwin stands alone as the nation's priciest market; Sydney and the NSW cluster anchor the affordable end.
