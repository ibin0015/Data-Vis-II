Where does it come from?

Every cup starts with a long journey

Before any Australian lifts a flat white to their lips, that coffee has already crossed oceans. Australia imports green coffee beans from over 50 countries, but the supply is far from evenly spread. A handful of nations — led by Brazil, Colombia and Honduras — supply the vast majority of what Australia drinks.

The map below shows exactly where those beans come from. The darker the country, the more it exports to Australia. What you see is a story of dependence: a dense band across Latin America provides over 60% of all Australian coffee imports, with East Africa and South-East Asia making up most of the rest.

This matters because it creates vulnerability. A drought, a frost, or a political disruption in Minas Gerais, Brazil can drive up coffee prices felt in every café across Australia within a single season — and as we will see later, that is exactly what happened.

Not all coffee is the same

Two species dominate the global coffee trade, and they serve very different purposes. Arabica is the bean of choice for specialty cafés — it is mild, aromatic, and the foundation of the flat white and latte culture that defines Australian coffee. Robusta is cheaper, more bitter, and mostly ends up in instant coffee and commercial espresso blends.

Globally, Arabica makes up about 70% of total consumption — and Australia skews even higher than that average. The country's café scene is built almost entirely on Arabica, which is why events in Arabica-producing regions like Brazil and Colombia hit Australian prices so directly.

Brazil leads the world — by a wide margin

Brazil exports more green coffee than any other country — roughly 37,000 thousand 60 kg bags per year, or about 31% of the entire global supply. Vietnam is second with 24,600 thousand bags, mostly Robusta. Together, the top three countries — Brazil, Vietnam and Colombia — account for nearly 62% of all global coffee exports.

Further down the list, Ethiopia and Uganda represent Africa's strong Arabica heritage. These are the high-quality, single-origin beans increasingly found on Australian specialty café menus.

"A crop failure in Minas Gerais, Brazil can send coffee prices across the world surging within a season."

Who actually drinks it?

The map below shows total coffee consumption by country in 2023. The United States leads at 1.4 million tonnes, followed by Brazil — remarkably, also one of the world's biggest consumers of its own export — and Germany. Australia sits at around 47,000 tonnes, or about 1.8 kg per person per year. That number may seem modest compared to Europe, but it understates the cultural intensity of the Australian café scene, which is built on quality espresso rather than filter coffee volume.

The latte leads, but the flat white defines us

Walk into any Australian café and the menu tells the same story: milk-based espresso drinks dominate. The latte is the most popular order at around 22–23%, but the flat white — invented in Australia and now served in coffee chains across London and New York — is right behind it.

The flat white is Australia's coffee identity. It is smaller and stronger than a latte, with a higher coffee-to-milk ratio, and it requires more barista skill to get right. Its popularity reflects a café culture that genuinely values quality over convenience.

At the other end of the chart, espresso and cortado remain niche despite their deep roots in Italian-Australian communities — a sign that for most Australians, coffee is still a milk drink first.

Where Australia's cafés are

Each dot on the map below represents the café and restaurant count for a state, sized by the number of venues. The picture is striking: New South Wales and Victoria together account for over 60% of all venues in the country. The density reflects where Australia's population — and its café culture — is most concentrated. The Northern Territory's tiny dot, by contrast, shows how thinly spread café culture becomes once you leave the major population centres.

How search behaviour tells the story of coffee culture

Google Trends data from 2004 to 2026 tracks how often Australians search for "coffee" and "café" online. The trend reveals something important: interest in coffee has not just stayed steady — it has grown. From around 2015 onward, search volume increased noticeably, coinciding with the rise of specialty coffee culture and third-wave cafés across the country. The dip in 2020 during COVID lockdowns is clearly visible, when cafés closed and the ritual moved home. But recovery was swift — within a year, search interest had returned to pre-pandemic levels, suggesting that for most Australians, the café experience is not easily replaced.

What does it cost?

Who is drinking the most caffeine?

The bubble chart below looks at caffeine intake from sweetened beverages by age group. The size of each bubble shows that group's share of total caffeine consumption. Young adults aged 18–29 consume the most, at around 21 mg per day from sweetened sources. But the data also shows something less expected: teenagers consume more caffeine than many assume, largely through energy drinks and sweetened coffee products rather than traditional espresso. Older Australians, by contrast, tend to get their caffeine from plain brewed coffee — a flat white or long black — rather than sweetened drinks.

The rising cost of green coffee

The price of raw green coffee beans, converted to Australian dollars, has been one of the most volatile commodity stories of the past 50 years. Three major events stand out clearly in the chart.

The 1977 Brazilian frost wiped out a large portion of the world's coffee crop, sending prices to record highs almost overnight. The 1997 Asian demand boom drove another surge as rapidly growing economies began drinking more coffee. Then came the 2011 global commodity spike, which pushed prices to levels not seen since the 1970s.

But the most significant development is the long-run trend: a crop that cost under $1 AUD per pound in the early 2000s now routinely exceeds $5. Use the navigator at the bottom of the chart to zoom into any time period.

What a flat white costs depends on where you live

The flat white is Australia's benchmark coffee — and its price is not the same everywhere. The chart on the left shows each state's average flat white price as a deviation from the national average of $4.99.

The Northern Territory is the most expensive, at $5.34 — nearly $0.45 above New South Wales, which is the cheapest at $4.89. The NT premium is not about coffee snobbery. It reflects the simple economics of remote supply chains: higher freight costs, fewer competitors, and a smaller customer base spreading fixed costs across fewer cups sold each day.

Victoria sits exactly at the national average — fitting for the state most associated with defining Australia's coffee culture. It is neither the cheapest nor the most expensive, but the standard against which others are measured.

City by city: how flat white prices cluster by state

Zoom further in and an interesting pattern emerges. Within each state, city prices are remarkably consistent — Brisbane, Gold Coast, Sunshine Coast and Cairns all sit at exactly $5.03. Adelaide, Barossa Valley and Victor Harbor all share $5.13. This consistency tells us that pricing is driven by state-level factors — wages, rent, supply logistics — rather than individual city dynamics. The one clear outlier is Darwin and Alice Springs in the NT, which stand alone at $5.35, well above every other city in the country. Hover over any dot to see the city name and price.

From a farm in Brazil to a cup in Carlton

The story of Australian coffee is ultimately a story about connection. Every flat white served in a Melbourne laneway café begins somewhere far away — most likely a plantation in Minas Gerais, Brazil, where the same soil that fuelled the 1977 price crisis is now the source of record-high 2024 prices.

Along the way, the bean passes through a global supply chain worth hundreds of millions of dollars, arrives in a country that has built an entire cultural identity around how it is prepared and served, and ends up in the hands of someone who will pay $5 for it without a second thought — until the price suddenly goes up.

That price, as this data shows, is not set by the café. It is set by weather patterns, trade volumes, exchange rates, and freight costs stretching back across the supply chain. Understanding that chain is what turns a simple coffee order into a data story.
